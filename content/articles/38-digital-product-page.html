The template

This is a copy-and-layout template — prose and structure you fill in and then build with whatever your theme already gives you. It is not a Shopify theme, an app, or a Liquid section. There is nothing to install.

Twelve sections, in the order a buyer needs them:

#SectionThe question it closes
1Identity and headlineWhat will I be able to do?
2Short descriptionIs this for someone like me?
3Three concrete benefitsWhy this one?
4Price and purchase actionWhat does it cost, and how do I buy it?
5Genuine previewIs it any good?
6Deliverables and formatsWhat lands in my downloads folder?
7First-use walkthroughWhat happens in the first twenty minutes?
8RequirementsCan I actually use this?
9Licence and deliveryWhat am I allowed to do with it?
10Support and limitationsWhat does it not do?
11FAQsThe three things still nagging me.
12Final purchase actionRight — where's the button?

Two rules decide whether this works. Every claim must be checkable by someone who has not bought it — if you cannot point at where a number came from, cut it. And requirements go before benefits, because a buyer who discovers the prerequisite after paying is a support ticket at best.

The eight questions a buyer is actually asking

A digital product page has a harder job than a physical one. Nobody can pick the thing up. Every signal a buyer would normally get from handling an object has to be replaced by something you wrote. These are the eight:

  1. What problem does this solve? Stated as they would state it, not as your feature list.
  2. Who is it for? Specific enough that some readers correctly exclude themselves.
  3. What exactly will I receive? File types, counts, formats, size.
  4. What does a useful output look like? The one most pages skip.
  5. What do I need already? Software, runtime, access, skill.
  6. How does delivery work? When the file arrives and where.
  7. What does the licence allow? Especially client work.
  8. What support is included? Honestly, including "none".

Question 4 is the one that separates pages that sell from pages that describe. Most digital product pages answer "how much is there" — a folder tree, a file count, a word count — and never answer "is it any good". Those are different questions, and only the second one is being asked at the moment somebody decides.

A section filled in

Here is section 5 — the preview — written for a real product, the Conversion & Revenue Optimization Toolkit sold on this site. Watch what happens to the feature inventory.

The inventory version, which is what most pages ship:

Thirteen modules, 70 workflows, 68 commands, six report templates and four worked examples. 65,000 words of plain Markdown.

The same facts, turned into an explanation of use:

Every finding this toolkit produces carries an evidence line and an honesty label — one of proven bug, strong heuristic, experiment opportunity, or insufficient data. That last label is the point. A finding marked insufficient data is the workflow telling you it cannot support a conclusion, which is the thing a confident-sounding audit will never say.

The experimentation module works the same way: before it designs a test it checks whether your traffic could detect the effect you are looking for, and if it cannot, it says so and stops rather than producing a test that will read as noise.

The numbers did not disappear — they moved to section 6, where "how much" belongs. What replaced them is a mechanism a buyer can evaluate. The counts are still true and still verifiable; they are just no longer doing a job they were never able to do.

What did not get added: a refund guarantee, lifetime updates, included support, or a revenue outcome. None of those is true of this product, and the fact that they would all improve the paragraph is exactly why the rule about checkable claims has to be mechanical rather than a matter of judgement in the moment.

The before state is worth sitting with, because it is not a design failure — it is a structural one. Somebody reading the whole page, persuaded, arriving at the bottom, was offered a different product and no way to buy the one they had just read about. That is a template decision that survived because nobody looked at the rendered page from the bottom up.

Note also what the "after" requirement line does: it actively sends some readers away, to a cheaper product, before they buy. That is not generosity. A buyer with no traffic who buys a conversion toolkit is a refund request, a bad review, or a customer who never returns.

Showing the output, not the packaging

Pick the artefact your product produces that a stranger could judge in thirty seconds. Ranked by how well they usually work:

  1. A finished output, rendered as readable text. A completed report, a filled-in template, a worked example. Best, because it is the actual thing.
  2. An annotated excerpt. Two hundred words from a module with a note on what it is doing. Good when the full artefact is long.
  3. A real screenshot of real output. Fine, but keep the substance as text too — an image is not readable by a search engine, a screen reader, or somebody on a phone.
  4. A folder tree. Only as a supplement. It answers "how much", never "is it good".

Three things not to do. Do not fabricate a dashboard or a UI the product does not have. Do not show a results chart for results nobody measured. And do not put the load-bearing information only inside an image — the sentence that convinces someone should be selectable text.

A practical constraint worth respecting: publish the smallest useful excerpt. The preview exists to let someone judge quality, not to substitute for buying. One report out of six, one worked example out of four.

Checks before you call it done

The purchase-path ones cost money when they fail, so they go first.

  • Every purchase control sells the same thing. View source and count distinct variant ids. There should be exactly one.
  • The closing action is this product. Not the flagship, not the bundle.
  • Labels match behaviour. Add to cart adds; Buy now enters checkout.
  • The price is rendered, not typed. A price in the description body is wrong the first time you change it.
  • In-page links stay on the page. A "What's included" link pointing at /#included goes to the homepage. From a product page that is a navigation away from the sale.
  • The refund line matches the policy. Open both and compare the words — this is the most common contradiction on a digital product page, and it is usually caused by the policy moving after the description was written.
  • No horizontal scroll at 390px. Tables and code blocks scroll inside their own container.
  • One h1. If the theme renders the product title as the h1, the description must not contain another.
  • One Product structured-data block, price matching the visible price, and no aggregateRating unless real reviews exist.
  • Readable with JavaScript off. The offer, the requirements and the price should all survive it.

Two of these have one-line checks. Distinct variant ids:

curl -s https://yourstore.com/products/your-handle \
  | grep -o 'name="id" value="[0-9]*"' | sort -u

# more than one line = two controls disagree about what they sell

And anchors that leave the page:

curl -s https://yourstore.com/products/your-handle \
  | grep -oE 'href="/#[a-z-]+"'

# anything returned is a link to the HOMEPAGE's section, from a product page

Seven mistakes worth naming

MistakeWhy it costs
"The ultimate all-in-one toolkit"Says nothing a competitor could not copy verbatim. Occupies the position where the outcome should be.
A file count as the headlineAnswers "how much" to a reader still asking "what is it".
Prerequisites hidden in an accordionThe buyer finds out after paying. Your refund policy then decides how expensive that was.
A generic purchase block linking to the wrong productUsually a shared template with a hard-coded product. Invisible in the template, obvious in the rendered page.
Testimonials or scarcity you cannot supportFabricated social proof is a legal exposure, not a conversion tactic, and countdowns that reset teach returning visitors to distrust the page.
Wording that implies a hosted service"Automatically audits your site" for a product that is Markdown files sets up a refund. Say what runs, and who runs it.
A refund summary that contradicts the policyThe page and the policy disagree, and the customer reads whichever helps them. Link, don't paraphrase.

A prompt for drafting the revision

This reads the product and the theme, drafts, and stops. It publishes nothing.

Draft a revised Shopify product page for a downloadable product, using the
template structure I will paste below. Draft only. Publish nothing.

FIRST, READ — do not write anything yet
- Read the product record: title, handle, variants, price, existing
  description, media, SEO fields, metafields.
- Read the theme template and sections that render a product page.
- Read the store's refund policy, shipping policy and licence page.
- Read the actual downloadable contents if I have given you a path to them.
- Tell me which of the template's twelve sections the current page already
  answers, and which it does not. Wait for me before drafting.

WHAT YOU MUST NOT CHANGE
- Price, compare-at price, currency.
- Product id, handle, variant ids, SKU.
- Inventory, fulfilment, digital delivery configuration.
- Licence terms or refund eligibility.
- Any product other than the one I named.

FACTS
- Use only facts from the product record, the theme, the store policies, or
  the product files themselves.
- Do not add a guarantee, a support commitment, an update promise, a
  customer count, a testimonial, a time saving or a revenue outcome.
- If a template section needs a fact you cannot find, write
  "NEEDS FACT: " and move on. Do not fill the gap.
- Quote the refund position from the store policy, and link to it rather
  than paraphrasing it.

THE DRAFT
- Follow the twelve sections in order.
- Requirements must appear before the second purchase action and must not be
  inside a collapsed element.
- Reuse the theme's EXISTING product form for every purchase control. Do not
  write a new add-to-cart. Every control must resolve the variant from the
  product, never from a hard-coded id.
- Render the price from the product. Never write a price into the copy.
- The closing section must sell THIS product. Any cross-sell goes after it,
  styled quieter, labelled as a different product.
- Do not emit a second h1 if the theme already renders the product title as
  one.

VALIDATE BEFORE YOU HAND IT BACK
- Count distinct variant ids in your draft. Report the number.
- List every in-page anchor and confirm each target exists in your draft.
- Confirm no hard-coded price string.
- Confirm the refund wording matches the policy you read.
- List every factual claim you made and, beside each, where it came from.

OUTPUT
The draft as a file I can review, plus the validation report and the list of
NEEDS FACT items. Do not update the product. Do not publish. Do not push to
a theme.

Questions

Is this a Shopify theme or section I can install?

No. It is a writing and structure template. There is no file to install and nothing is generated. You build the sections with whatever your theme already provides.

How long should a digital product page be?

Long enough to answer the eight questions and no longer. A page that answers them in six hundred words is finished. Length is a symptom, not a goal — the useful test is whether a reader could still be uncertain about delivery, requirements or licence after reading it.

Should I put the price near the top?

Yes. Hiding it does not prevent the objection, it just moves it later and adds irritation. If the price needs justifying, justify it next to the price.

What if my product has no output to preview?

Then produce one. Run your own workflow on a fictional example, label it as fictional, and publish that. If you cannot produce a single artefact worth showing, that is worth knowing before you spend more on the page.

Do I need testimonials?

Not if you do not have real ones. An honest limitation section does more for credibility than an unattributable quote, and it cannot be challenged.

Next action

Open your product page and scroll to the bottom. Look at the last call to action: is it for the product on the page, and does it name the price? On a surprising number of stores the answer is no, and it is the cheapest thing on this list to fix.
